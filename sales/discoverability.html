One-pager · for compliance & counsel

"Isn't an immutable record of near-misses just plaintiff ammunition?"

It's the first question every Chief Compliance Officer asks about decision evidence. It deserves a direct answer, not a dodge. Here it is.

Informational, not legal advice. Retention and access design should be confirmed with your own counsel against your applicable obligations.

1. Retention is configurable — align it to the rule, not to "forever."

Decision evidence is not an immutable-forever archive by default. Retention is a configuration. For broker-dealers, the natural setting aligns to the 31 CFR 1023.320(d) five-year window; banks set theirs to their applicable record-keeping obligation. The chain is tamper-evident (you can prove a kept record wasn't altered) — it is not tamper-proof against a lawful, logged retention policy that ages records out on schedule.

2. Access is need-to-know, and the access itself is audited.

The ledger is not a firm-wide broadcast of every near-miss. Access is scoped to the people who need it, and every read is itself recorded. That is the posture examiners expect for sensitive compliance material, and it is the opposite of the uncontrolled spreadsheet the evidence replaces.

3. The decisive point: the absence of the record is the liability.

The $7.5M Merrill penalty was for the absence of a below-the-line disposition record — not for having one. Unrecorded rationale is what regulators sanction. A firm that can hand an examiner a complete, integrity-verified disposition ledger is in a categorically stronger position than one reconstructing a suppressed population from memory under a consent order. The record is the defense, not the exposure.

In one sentence

Configurable retention aligned to the 5-year rule, need-to-know access with an access audit, and the reminder that the fine was for the missing record — so the defensible choice is to keep it, well.
